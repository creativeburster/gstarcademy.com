Finite Element Analysis (FEA) Fundamentals

FEA basics: meshing, boundary conditions, solver types, convergence, and result interpretation for structural analysis.

By Gstarcademy Editorial Team Updated 2026-07-28 Editorial process

Definition & Context

Finite Element Analysis (FEA) is a numerical method that subdivides a continuous body into a finite number of discrete elements connected at nodes, then solves the governing partial differential equations (equilibrium, heat balance) approximately across that mesh. In structural analysis the core equation is [K]{u} = {F} — the global stiffness matrix multiplied by the nodal displacement vector equals the applied load vector.

A complete FEA study has three stages: pre-processing (geometry cleanup, meshing, material assignment, boundary conditions and loads), solving (assembling and inverting the stiffness matrix — direct sparse solvers for small models, iterative PCG for large ones), and post-processing (von Mises stress, displacement, factor of safety, reaction forces). Common solvers include Nastran, Abaqus, ANSYS Mechanical, and the embedded solvers in SOLIDWORKS Simulation and Fusion.

Why It Matters

FEA lets engineers validate designs against yield, fatigue, buckling, and thermal limits before cutting metal, replacing expensive physical prototypes. Understanding element theory, mesh quality, and boundary-condition idealisation is what separates a trustworthy result from a colourful but meaningless stress plot.

The discipline that makes FEA trustworthy is verification and validation: a mesh-convergence study (refining until the quantity of interest stops changing) confirms the result is solver-accurate, while comparison to a hand calculation or test confirms the model represents reality. The most common errors are not in the solver but in idealisation — over-constrained supports that create artificial stiffness, point loads/restraints that produce meaningless singular stresses at re-entrant corners, and assuming linear-elastic behaviour where contact, large deflection, or plasticity actually govern.

Best Practices

  • Always run a mesh convergence study: refine the mesh until peak stress changes by less than ~5% between refinements, otherwise your result depends on element size.
  • Use second-order (parabolic) elements for stress analysis — linear tetrahedra are overly stiff and under-predict deflection.
  • Idealise boundary conditions to match reality: over-constraining introduces artificial stiffness and hides real deflection.
  • Check reaction forces sum to the applied load — a quick sanity check that constraints and loads balance.
  • Interpret stress at singularities (sharp re-entrant corners, point loads) with caution; use stress linearisation or add fillets rather than reading the infinite peak.

Common Pitfalls

  • Reading peak stress at a geometric singularity (sharp corner) as a real value — it rises without bound as the mesh refines.
  • Applying loads or restraints to a single node/vertex, creating artificial stress concentrations.
  • Ignoring units — mixing mm and N with GPa vs MPa is the most common source of wildly wrong results.
  • Trusting a coarse single-run mesh with no convergence check.
  • Using linear-elastic assumptions where the material actually yields (needs a nonlinear plasticity model).
