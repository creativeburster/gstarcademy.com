Scallop Cusp Height & CAM Stepover Optimization

Mathematical relationship between ball-nose cutter radius, stepover distance, and scallop cusp height. Achieve specified Ra surface finishes in 3D machining.

By Gstarcademy Editorial Team Updated 2026-10-12 Editorial process

1. Definition & Engineering Significance

In 3-axis and 5-axis CNC surface finishing, machining curved organic surfaces with ball-nose or bull-nose endmills creates microscopic ridges between adjacent passes, known as scallop cusp height ($h$). Scallop height directly governs the aerodynamic drag, cosmetic reflectivity, and post-machining polishing labor required for injection molds, stamping dies, and aerospace airfoil surfaces. Understanding the exact mathematical relationship between cutter radius ($R$), stepover distance ($a_e$), and cusp height ($h$) is essential for optimizing cycle time and finish quality.

2. Technical Architecture & Key Parameters

  • Cusp Height Formula: For a ball-nose endmill of radius $R$ traversing a flat or gentle surface with stepover $a_e$, the mathematical cusp height is:
    h = R − √[R² − (ae / 2)²] ≈ ae² / (8R)
  • Stepover Calculation: Inverting the formula to achieve a target cusp height $h$:
    ae = √(8 · R · h)
  • Surface Roughness ($R_a$) Correlation: Theoretical arithmetic mean surface roughness $R_a$ correlates to approximately $R_a \approx 0.25 \times h$ under ideal rigid cutting conditions.
  • Cycle Time vs Finish Tradeoff: Halving the scallop height requires reducing stepover by approximately $\sqrt{2}$ (41%), doubling overall CNC machine run time.

3. Industry Best Practices & Failure Modes

In injection mold manufacturing, specifying an excessively tiny scallop height (e.g., 0.0005 mm) creates millions of G-code blocks, bloating file sizes and causing CNC buffer starvation that results in stuttering and surface witness marks. Conversely, an overly coarse stepover leaves tall scallops that require hours of expensive manual bench polishing by skilled toolmakers, risking geometric deviation from CAD nominals.

4. Production Governance & Standard Protocols

Modern high-end CAM systems implement constant cusp (or 3D scallop) toolpaths that automatically compute local surface slope, tightening stepover on steep vertical walls and expanding it on gentle planar slopes. Using barrel-cutters or circle-segment endmills with huge effective radii ($R=50$ mm to $250$ mm) allows CAM programmers to slash cycle times by 80% while retaining ultra-low cusp heights.
