Topology Optimization & Generative Design

Topology optimization algorithms that remove material from a design space while maintaining structural requirements.

By Gstarcademy Editorial Team Updated 2026-07-28 Editorial process

Definition & Context

Topology optimisation mathematically removes material from a design space to minimise an objective (usually compliance = maximise stiffness) subject to a mass or volume-fraction constraint. The dominant method is SIMP (Solid Isotropic Material with Penalisation), which treats each element's density as a design variable between 0 and 1 and penalises intermediate densities toward solid/void.

The optimiser iterates FEA + sensitivity analysis to grow load-bearing 'bone-like' structures. Outputs are organic and typically require geometry reconstruction (smoothing to a manufacturable solid or lattice) before verification FEA. Tools include Altair OptiStruct/Inspire, Ansys, nTop, Fusion Generative Design, and SOLIDWORKS Topology.

Why It Matters

Topology optimisation delivers minimum-weight parts that meet stiffness/strength targets — critical for aerospace, motorsport, and additive manufacturing where every gram costs fuel or money.

The result is only a starting point, not a finished part: the raw SIMP output is a rough density field that must be reconstructed into clean, manufacturable geometry and then re-verified with a fresh FEA, because the smoothing step changes stiffness and stress. Results also depend heavily on the setup — the design space, retained (frozen) regions, load cases, and the volume-fraction target — and the organic shapes it produces usually assume additive manufacturing or casting; forcing them into a machined part often erases the weight savings, which is why manufacturing constraints (minimum member size, symmetry, draw/overhang direction) belong in the optimisation itself.

Best Practices

  • Define the design and non-design (keep-out) regions carefully — interfaces and bearing surfaces must stay.
  • Include manufacturing constraints (minimum member size, draw direction, symmetry) so the result is producible.
  • Reconstruct and smooth the raw result, then run a verification FEA on the manufacturable geometry.
  • For AM, consider lattice infill and self-supporting angles in the optimisation.
  • Set realistic load cases and combine them; optimising for one load can fail another.

Common Pitfalls

  • Treating the raw density field as the final part without manufacturable reconstruction.
  • Optimising a single load case and ignoring others (fatigue, secondary loads).
  • Omitting manufacturing constraints, producing shapes only AM can build.
  • Skipping the verification FEA on the smoothed geometry.
